\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}

\subsubsection{FeedbackQA} 

The \textit{FeedbackQA} dataset \citet{li_using_2022} contains evaluations of Q\&A pairs ($n=5660$). Q\&A pairs related to Covid-19 were scraped from the websites of WHO, US Government, UK Government, Canadian government, and the Australian government. Crowd-source workers from English-speaking countries were hired through Amazon's MTurk platform to rate each Q\&A pair and provide feedback. The rating is performed on a 4-point likert scale (\textit{bad, could be improved, good, excellent}). The feedback concerns "a natural language explanation elaborating on the strengths and/or weaknesses of the answer" \citep{li_using_2022}/ Since each Q\&A pair contains two ratings, we aggregate both into the mean to obtain our "ground truth" target variable. 

\subsubsection{Barter deals}

The \textit{Barter Deals} dataset contains 'deals' written by companies ($n=???$). On the Barter platform, companies ('partners') can barter goods and services in exchange for influencer content. For example, a company that sells hair styling tools offers their new product in exchange for a video of a creator using it; or, a museum provides free entrance to a creator and their friends in exchange for a video of their visit. The 'deal text' is the marketing copy that is posted on the Barter platform. Users can apply to deals, and the partner can accept or reject the application. Once accepted, the partner provides the good or service, and the creator provides the content. 

In the current study, we consider the 'deal text' (marketing copy) and the number of applications the deal has obtained after the first seven days of going live (\textit{applications after 7 days}). The variable \textit{applications after 7 days} is chosen because it serves as a proxy for marketing copy quality. One might apply to a deal because they like the product or the brand, but they could also be convinced to apply bceause of the way the copy is written. A valid measurement of marketing copy quality would therefore be predictive of whether somebody applies to a deal or not. By controlling for the fixed effects of the brand and the product category we can separate the effects of marketing copy quality, and hereby determine whether the LLM provides a valid measurement of it. 

\subsection{Operationalization of Evaluation Constructs}

To empirically measure the efficacy of the formative prompting framework, we define an experimental condition and a control condition (baseline). In the experimental condition (\textit{formative prompt}) we treat the construct as a formative variable by decomposing it into sub-dimensions (e.g., $d_1, d_2, d_3$). We prompt the LLM to measure the subdimensions individually, yielding multiple scalar values. In the control condition (\textit{holistic prompt}) we treat the construct as a monolith, forcing the model to implicitly aggregate the dimensions into a single scalar rating $Y$. 

\subsubsection{Construct definition: FeedbackQA}

For the question answering task, we derive the formative dimensions from the qualitative analysis of the feedback provided in \citet{li_using_2022}. The authors identify numerous criteria that define "answer quality" by examining the explanations crowd-sourced workers provided for their ratings. From this, we derive three dimensions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relevance:} The extent to which the answer avoids irrelevant content
    \item \textbf{Completeness:} Whether the answer covers all aspects of the query.
    \item \textbf{Directness:} Whether the answer directly answers the question.
\end{itemize}

We expect a degree of covariance among the dimensions, since high-quality answers generally score high across all three. However, the formative framework allows for orthogonal variation across the dimensions as well. For example, \citet{li_using_2022} highlight an answer rated \textit{could be improved} (2/4) with the feedback: \textit{"The answer is about some of the online risks but not about how to protect against them."}. This indicates an answer that is relevant but incomplete. While a holistic prompt forces the model to compress the conflicting signals into a single scalar value, the formative prompt explicitly models models this by decomposing it.

\subsection{Model selection}

To perform evaluations we select three popular small-scale, open-source instruction-tuned models: Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 \citep{qwen3technicalreport} (AliBaba), Gemma3-4B-IT \citep{gemma_2025} (Google), and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Meta). These models were selected to represent the current state-of-the-art open-source models. 

The choice of model type (small, open-source, instruction-tuned) is based on the methodological requirements, and the operational constraints of the industrial partner, Barter. Methodologically, the use of open-source models enables "white-box" access to the model's probability distribution. This allows us to compute the logit-weighted mean and the Shannon entropy. Moreover, we prefer instruction-tuned models over base models to ensure adherence to the evaluation instructions provided in the prompt. Operationally, Barter requires the evaluator to balance validity with cost-efficiency and latency. Proprietary models like GPT-4 incur costs that scale with volume, while open-source models incur only fixed compute costs, making them a more scalable solution.
